Chapter 1 · Foundations

What is temperature, really?

Before anything else, we need to understand what temperature actually is — not what a thermometer reads, but what physical reality it describes.

Imagine a box of air. Inside the box are roughly 1022 molecules, each one moving in a random direction at some speed. They collide with each other and with the walls constantly. At any given moment, the speeds are wildly different — some molecules are nearly at rest after a collision, others are moving very fast.

Temperature is a single number that summarises the average kinetic energy of all those molecules. Nothing more, nothing less. A "hot" gas is one where the molecules are moving faster on average. A "cold" gas has slower molecules.

Key insight

Temperature and energy are two descriptions of the same physical fact. Temperature is the macroscopic, human-readable version. Kinetic energy is the microscopic, per-molecule reality. They are not different phenomena — they are different languages for the same thing.

The problem: two different units for the same thing

Here is the puzzle. Energy is measured in Joules [J]. Temperature is measured in Kelvin [K]. These are completely different units, historically defined by completely different experimental procedures — Kelvin by the behaviour of gases, Joules by mechanics.

Yet they describe the same physical quantity. So there must be a conversion factor between them. That conversion factor is Boltzmann's constant, kB.

Chapter 2 · Foundations

Boltzmann's constant kB

Boltzmann's constant converts between temperature (in Kelvin) and thermal energy (in Joules) at the level of a single particle:

Boltzmann's constant kB = 1.380649 × 10−23 J/K kB — Boltzmann's constant [J/K] Exact value since 2019 SI redefinition

The number is tiny — on the order of 10−23 — because a single molecule carries an almost unimaginably small amount of energy. At room temperature (T ≈ 300 K), the thermal energy per degree of freedom per molecule is:

Thermal energy scale at room temperature ½ kBT ≈ ½ × 1.38×10−23 × 300 ≈ 2×10−21 J This is about 12 milli-electron-volts — tiny, but not zero.

Why is kB so small?

The Kelvin scale was defined long before anyone understood molecules. People agreed that water freezes at 273.15 K and boils at 373.15 K — based on human-scale observations. But one kelvin of temperature difference, experienced by a single molecule, corresponds to an absurdly small kinetic energy.

kB is the bridge between those two worlds. It is small because we happened to define temperature on a human scale rather than a molecular scale.

Connection to the gas constant

You may have seen R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), the gas constant. This is just kB scaled up to one mole of particles: R = NA kB, where NA = 6.022×1023 mol−1 is Avogadro's number. Same physics — different counting unit. Historically, Perrin measured kB via Brownian motion in 1908 and got NA = 6.4×1023 — within 6% of the correct value. That's what you're replicating.

Chapter 3 · Foundations

The equipartition theorem

Now that we have the conversion factor kB, we need a statement about how much energy each particle actually carries. That is the equipartition theorem.

The theorem: In thermal equilibrium at temperature T, every quadratic degree of freedom in the energy carries a mean energy of ½kBT.

What is a "quadratic degree of freedom"? It is any term in the energy that is proportional to a coordinate or velocity squared. For a free particle moving in 3D, the kinetic energy is:

Kinetic energy in 3D E = ½mvx² + ½mvy² + ½mvz² m — particle mass [kg] vx, vy, vz — velocity components [m/s]

Each term is quadratic in a velocity. So by equipartition:

Equipartition theorem applied ⟨½mvx²⟩ = ½kBT    (and same for y, z) The angle brackets ⟨…⟩ mean "average over time" (or equivalently, over the ensemble).

This is a profound result. It says: the temperature T of a fluid sets the mean kinetic energy of every particle in it, regardless of mass, shape, or size. A water molecule and your 1 µm microsphere, both immersed in the same fluid at the same temperature, both have ½kBT of kinetic energy per direction.

Why does the sphere move at all?

This is exactly the answer. The water molecules in your sample cell are at room temperature T ≈ 295 K. By equipartition, each water molecule has mean kinetic energy ½kBT per direction. When they collide with the polystyrene sphere, they transfer momentum to it. The sphere therefore also ends up with mean kinetic energy ½kBT per direction — it is just another object in thermal equilibrium. That is why it moves. The motion is thermally driven.

Important: particle mass drops out

Since every particle has the same energy ½kBT regardless of mass, a heavier particle moves more slowly. The sphere is 109 times heavier than a water molecule, so it moves 104.5 times more slowly — but it still moves. This is why Brownian motion is visible under a microscope: the motion is slow enough to track, but it's real thermal energy causing it.

Chapter 4 · The Motion

Brownian motion — what it is

In 1827, botanist Robert Brown noticed that pollen grains suspended in water jittered under his microscope — a continuous, irregular, never-stopping dance. He first thought the grains were alive. Testing with inorganic dust particles revealed the same motion. The cause remained a mystery for 80 years.

Einstein solved it in 1905. His insight was this: the pollen grain is being bombarded from all sides by water molecules, and those bombardments are not perfectly balanced at any instant. The net random force from billions of asymmetric collisions per second sets the grain in motion.

At any time step, the net force has a random direction and magnitude — it could push the sphere slightly left, right, up, or down, with equal probability. Over time, the sphere traces out a jagged, irregular path with no preferred direction.

Three key observations

1. The motion never stops. As long as T > 0, the water molecules are moving and collisions are happening. Brownian motion is perpetual — it is not friction-less motion, it is thermally-driven motion.

2. No preferred direction. The average displacement ⟨Δr⟩ = 0. The particle goes left as often as right. The mean position does not drift (unless there is an external force or fluid flow — more on this when we discuss drift).

3. The typical distance grows as √t. If you watch the particle for time t, the typical distance from its starting point scales as √t, not t. This is the hallmark of diffusion and random walks — we will derive it precisely in the next chapter.

Chapter 5 · The Motion

The Langevin equation

To get a quantitative description, we write Newton's second law for the sphere. Two forces act on it:

Force 1 — Drag

When the sphere moves at velocity v, the surrounding fluid resists with force Fdrag = −γv. The minus sign: drag always opposes motion. The drag coefficient γ = 6πηr (Stokes law — explained in Chapter 7). γ has units [kg/s].

Force 2 — Random kicks

The net force from all the asymmetric molecular collisions at each instant. We call this F(t). It has zero mean (⟨F(t)⟩ = 0 — no preferred direction) and it changes on extremely short timescales (nanoseconds). It is uncorrelated in time: a kick at time t tells you nothing about the kick at time t+ε for any ε larger than the collision timescale.

Newton's second law F = ma gives:

The Langevin equation m(dv/dt) = −γv + F(t) m — sphere mass [kg] v — velocity [m/s] γ — drag coefficient [kg/s] F(t) — random force [N], ⟨F(t)⟩ = 0

This is a stochastic differential equation — an equation of motion with a random term. It is the foundational equation of Brownian motion theory.

The overdamped limit — why mass drops out

Look at the timescale on which the sphere "remembers" its velocity. If you give the sphere a kick, how long before the drag erases that velocity? The answer is the momentum relaxation time:

Momentum relaxation timescale τm = m/γ ≈ 10−7 s (for a 1 µm sphere in water) m ≈ 10−15 kg for a 1 µm polystyrene sphere  ·  γ ≈ 10−8 kg/s in water

Your camera captures frames every ~0.1 s. Between two frames, the particle has been re-randomised approximately 106 times. The velocity at frame n tells you absolutely nothing about the velocity at frame n+1 — it has been completely scrambled.

This means the inertial term m(dv/dt) is completely negligible at the timescales you observe. The Langevin equation simplifies to:

Overdamped Langevin equation γ(dx/dt) = F(t)   →   dx = [F(t)/γ] dt The particle position changes only due to the random kicks — inertia is irrelevant. Mass m has vanished entirely.
Physical meaning

The sphere has no "coasting." At each instant it is either being pushed or being dragged. The overdamped regime is the reason that the Einstein relation D = kBT/γ holds — it is the regime where friction and diffusion are directly linked, with no inertial complications.

Chapter 6 · The Motion

Random walks and the MSD

The simplest model: 1D drunk walk

Forget the physics for a moment. Imagine a person on a number line. Every second, they flip a coin: heads → step right (+ℓ), tails → step left (−ℓ). After N steps, where are they?

The total displacement is X = Δx1 + Δx2 + … + ΔxN. Because each step is ±ℓ with equal probability, the mean is ⟨X⟩ = 0 — the person ends up, on average, back where they started. But of course they do not stay at zero! They wander.

The right question: mean squared displacement

Since ⟨X⟩ = 0 is useless, we ask: what is ⟨X²⟩? Squaring eliminates the sign:

MSD derivation — 1D random walk ⟨X²⟩ = ⟨(Δx₁ + Δx₂ + … + ΔxN)²⟩ = ⟨Σᵢ Δxᵢ²⟩ + ⟨Σᵢ≠ⱼ Δxᵢ Δxⱼ⟩ = Nℓ² + 0 Cross terms ⟨Δxᵢ Δxⱼ⟩ = ⟨Δxᵢ⟩⟨Δxⱼ⟩ = 0 because steps are independent. Each squared term ⟨Δxᵢ²⟩ = ℓ² because each step is exactly ±ℓ.

So ⟨X²⟩ = Nℓ². The MSD grows linearly with the number of steps. This is purely a consequence of step independence — no physics assumed yet.

Converting to time: defining D

N steps in time t means N = t/τstep where τstep is the time per step. We define the diffusion coefficient D by:

Definition of diffusion coefficient D D ≡ ℓ² / (2τstep)    [m²/s] D encodes both the step size (ℓ) and how frequently steps occur (τstep). Large D: big steps, or fast steps.

Then in 1D: ⟨x²⟩ = Nℓ² = (t/τstep) × 2Dτstep = 2Dt. Generalising to d dimensions (x and y are independent, so their MSDs add):

MSD — central result of diffusion theory ⟨r²⟩ = 2dDt ⟨r²⟩ — mean squared displacement [m²] d — dimensionality (2 for your 2D tracking) D — diffusion coefficient [m²/s] t — lag time [s]

For your experiment: d = 2 (you track in the xy plane), so ⟨r²⟩ = 4Dt. A plot of MSD vs. lag time should be a straight line through the origin. The slope is 4D.

What MSD linearity tells you physically

The fact that ⟨r²⟩ ∝ t (linear in time) is the definition of normal diffusion. It is a direct consequence of each step being independent of the last. If the slope curves upward (MSD ∝ tα, α > 1), the particle has memory — perhaps it is being carried by a flow. If it curves downward (α < 1), the particle is being constrained — perhaps it is trapped near a surface. A straight line through the origin is your confirmation that you are seeing free, unhindered Brownian motion.

Chapter 7 · The Physics

The diffusion coefficient D

We defined D mathematically as ℓ²/2τ. But what determines D physically? This is where Einstein's genius comes in. He recognised that diffusion and friction are two faces of the same microscopic phenomenon.

Think about it: the same water molecule collisions that cause the sphere to diffuse also cause the sphere to decelerate when you push it. Friction and diffusion have the same origin. Einstein's fluctuation-dissipation theorem makes this precise:

Einstein relation (fluctuation-dissipation) D = kBT / γ D — diffusion coefficient [m²/s] kB — Boltzmann's constant [J/K] T — temperature [K] γ — drag coefficient [kg/s]

Read this carefully. Larger T → more thermal energy → larger D (more diffusion). Larger γ → more drag → smaller D (harder to move). The ratio kBT/γ captures the competition between thermal driving and viscous resistance.

Dimensional check

[kBT/γ] = [J/K × K] / [kg/s] = [J] / [kg/s] = [kg·m²/s²] / [kg/s] = [m²/s] ✓ — correct units for D.

Chapter 8 · The Physics

Stokes' law — drag on a sphere

To use the Einstein relation D = kBT/γ, we need an expression for the drag coefficient γ. For a sphere, this was solved by George Stokes in 1851.

When a sphere of radius r moves at velocity v through a viscous fluid of dynamic viscosity η [Pa·s], it experiences a drag force:

Stokes drag law Fdrag = −6πηr·v   →   γ = 6πηr η — dynamic viscosity [Pa·s = kg/(m·s)] r — sphere radius [m] γ = 6πηr — drag coefficient [kg/s]

What is viscosity η?

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to shear — its "thickness." Formally, η = (shear stress) / (velocity gradient) [Pa·s]. Water at 20°C has η ≈ 10−3 Pa·s. Glycerol is about 1000× more viscous. Honey is about 104× more viscous.

Why does drag scale linearly with r?

You might expect drag to scale as r² (cross-sectional area). It doesn't — it scales as r (linearly). The reason: Stokes drag is controlled by the velocity gradient in the fluid around the sphere, not by cross-sectional area. The velocity gradient length-scale is set by r, and the contact area is also set by r, so the two factors of r in "gradient × area" turn into r + r = 2r... actually, the full derivation shows that the viscous stress is ∝ η·v/r, and the area is ∝ r², giving a force ∝ η·v·r. The factor 6π comes from the exact solution to the Stokes equations around a sphere.

Assumptions behind Stokes' law

When Stokes law is valid

Low Reynolds number: Re = ρvr/η ≪ 1. For a 1 µm sphere in water moving at ~1 µm/s: Re ≈ 10−6. Deeply valid.

Rigid sphere: Polystyrene satisfies this. Drops or bubbles would deform and give a different factor.

Far from walls: Near a wall, the fluid has to squeeze through a narrower gap, increasing effective drag. If beads are near the coverslip surface, γ is larger than 6πηr. This can bias your D measurement downward and kB upward. Watch for beads stuck or rolling along walls.

η depends on temperature

This is critical for your week-2 runs. Water viscosity falls substantially with temperature:

Water viscosity (approximate values) η(5°C) ≈ 1.52 × 10⁻³ Pa·s  ·  η(20°C) ≈ 1.00 × 10⁻³ Pa·s  ·  η(25°C) ≈ 0.89 × 10⁻³ Pa·s Your pipeline computes η(T) automatically via physics.water_viscosity_Pa_s. Always use the computed value — never assume η = η(20°C).

Chapter 9 · The Physics

The Stokes-Einstein relation

Substitute Stokes' law γ = 6πηr into the Einstein relation D = kBT/γ:

The Stokes-Einstein relation — central equation of your experiment
D = kBT / (6πηr) D [m²/s] — measured from MSD slope kB [J/K] — what we want to extract T [K] — thermometer reading η [Pa·s] — looked up from tables (or computed) r [m] — from optical calibration

Every symbol except kB is either measured, looked up, or known. Rearranging:

Extracting Boltzmann's constant kB = 6πηr·D / T This is your measurement equation. Plug in your measured D, your known r, your measured T, and your looked-up η(T) — and you get kB.

Why this experiment can measure a fundamental constant

Stokes-Einstein connects a macroscopic, measurable quantity (D, from video tracking) to a microscopic fundamental constant (kB). The bridge is thermodynamics (equipartition) + hydrodynamics (Stokes drag). Einstein showed for the first time that watching a particle under a microscope lets you reach into the atomic world. That is why Perrin won the Nobel Prize for doing exactly this experiment.

Chapter 10 · The Experiment

Measuring D from your tracking data

You have a trajectory: a list of (x, y) positions at known times. Here is exactly how D is extracted.

Step 1 — Compute displacements at each lag time τ

For a lag time τ = n × Δt (where Δt = 1/fps ≈ 0.107 s for your camera), the squared displacement at each time window i is:

Squared displacement r²(i, τ) = [x(ti+τ) − x(ti)]² + [y(ti+τ) − y(ti)]²

Step 2 — Average over all windows of the same lag time

For a trajectory of N total frames, at lag time τ = nΔt, there are N−n windows. Average all of them:

Time-averaged MSD ⟨r²(τ)⟩ = (1/(N−n)) × Σᵢ r²(i, τ) This is the time-average that the ergodic hypothesis says equals the ensemble average.

Step 3 — Plot MSD vs. τ and fit a line

The MSD curve ⟨r²⟩ vs τ should be linear. Fit a line: ⟨r²⟩ = 4Dτ + offset. The slope is 4D, so:

Extracting D from the slope D = slope / 4 The offset (y-intercept) should be near zero. A large offset signals localisation noise — position measurement uncertainty that adds a constant to all MSD values regardless of lag time.
Only fit the linear regime

At very short lag times (1–2 frames), localisation noise dominates. At very long lag times, the trajectory has fewer windows — statistical noise increases, and drift can corrupt the result. A typical good fit range is roughly 5% to 30% of the total trajectory length in frames.

Chapter 11 · The Experiment

The ergodic hypothesis

The MSD formula ⟨r²⟩ = 4Dt is mathematically defined as an ensemble average: release 106 identical particles at t = 0 and average r² over all of them at time t. You cannot do this — you have one or a few particles tracked over time.

The ergodic hypothesis states: for a particle in thermal equilibrium, the time average equals the ensemble average, given sufficiently long observation time.

Ergodic hypothesis ⟨r²(τ)⟩time = ⟨r²(τ)⟩ensemble Only valid when: the trajectory is long enough, the system is in equilibrium, and there are no long-time correlations (e.g. the particle isn't drifting or getting stuck).

This is an assumption, not a proven theorem for real systems. It is well-justified for free Brownian particles in equilibrium, but it can fail in several physically important ways:

Short times — memory not lost

At τ ≲ τm = m/γ ≈ 10−7 s, the particle still "remembers" its initial velocity. The time average over a short trajectory does not sample all possible states. (Not relevant here — your shortest observable lag is much longer.)

Long times — sample cell changes

Over very long trajectories, the sample cell may evaporate slightly, the temperature may drift, or the bead may gradually approach a wall. The system is no longer in the same equilibrium state throughout, so the time average is not a valid ensemble average.

Drift — broken translation invariance

If a flow velocity is present, the particle's position is not translation-invariant over time. The ergodic hypothesis requires no preferred direction — drift violates this. This is why drift must be identified and handled before extracting D. (Next chapter.)

In practice, you use multiple beads and treat each trajectory as an approximately independent realisation. Pooling results from many beads brings you closer to a true ensemble average and reduces statistical uncertainty.

Chapter 12 · The Experiment

Drift — what it is, why it appears, how to handle it

What drift is physically

Drift is a systematic, constant (or slowly-varying) velocity superimposed on top of the random Brownian motion. Its sources in your experiment include:

Mechanical — sample cell flow

If the sample cell is not perfectly sealed, evaporation can cause slow fluid flow. Similarly, any tilt of the microscope stage causes gravitational sedimentation (though for 1 µm spheres in water this is slow). Convection currents from the microscope lamp heating the sample can also cause bulk flow.

Optical — stage drift

The microscope stage itself can drift mechanically over minutes, causing all particles in the field of view to translate together. This looks like all particles sharing the same drift velocity — a useful diagnostic.

Physical — thermal gradient

If the sample is not at uniform temperature (a gradient exists), particles can undergo thermophoresis — net motion toward the cooler region. This looks like drift directed along the temperature gradient.

What drift does to the MSD

Suppose a drift velocity vd = (vx, vy) is present. The total displacement at lag τ is:

Displacement with drift Δr(τ) = Δrdiffusion(τ) + vd·τ The diffusion term is random (zero mean). The drift term is systematic and grows linearly with τ.

Computing the MSD:

MSD with drift (expanded) ⟨|Δr(τ)|²⟩ = 4Dτ + |vd|²τ² 4Dτ — diffusion term, linear in τ |vd|²τ² — drift term, quadratic in τ

How to detect drift

Method 1 — Visual inspection of the MSD. If your MSD curves upward (super-linear) instead of being straight, drift is likely present. The curvature is more pronounced at long lag times because the τ² term grows faster than τ.

Method 2 — Check the mean displacement ⟨Δr⟩. Pure diffusion gives ⟨Δr⟩ = 0 in each direction. If ⟨Δx(τ)⟩ ≠ 0 or ⟨Δy(τ)⟩ ≠ 0 and these means grow linearly with τ, you have drift with velocity vd = d⟨Δr⟩/dτ.

Method 3 — Check multiple particles. If all particles in the field of view drift in the same direction at the same speed, it is stage drift or global flow. If particles drift in different directions, it is something more complex (convection cells, local flows).

How to correct for drift — the physics-based approach

The cleanest correction does not touch your raw data. Instead, you measure the drift velocity from the mean displacement:

Drift velocity from mean displacement vd,x = ⟨Δx(τ)⟩ / τ    vd,y = ⟨Δy(τ)⟩ / τ

Then subtract the drift contribution from the MSD at each lag time:

Drift-corrected MSD ⟨r²(τ)⟩corrected = ⟨|Δr(τ)|²⟩ − |vd|²τ² This removes the quadratic term, leaving the linear diffusion signal. Then fit 4Dτ to the corrected curve as before.
Why this is physics-based, not ad hoc

The decomposition Δr = Δrdiffusion + vdτ is exact if vd is constant during the trajectory. The subtraction is not "removing data" — it is correctly separating the deterministic drift from the stochastic diffusion. The physics guarantees that these two contributions to MSD are additive and independent, so subtracting the drift term is equivalent to observing the particle in a frame co-moving with the fluid.

When drift correction is not enough

If the drift is not constant — if it varies during the trajectory — then a simple linear subtraction underestimates the total drift contribution. In this case, you should reject the trajectory entirely, or restrict the fit to short lag times (where drift hasn't yet accumulated). The wall-hindered beads appearing above the fit line in your plot2 is a physical alarm related to this: beads near walls have reduced D (wall hydrodynamics), which makes their kB appear too high if r is also slightly overestimated.

Chapter 13 · The Experiment

Extracting kB — the full chain

Here is the complete measurement chain, from raw pixels to fundamental constant:

Step 1 — Calibrate space

Convert pixel positions to physical distances using the spatial calibration: mpp = 0.14381 µm/px (confirmed via multi-line grid detection). Note that kB ∝ mpp³ — a 5% calibration error causes a 15% error in kB. This is highly leveraged.

Step 2 — Track trajectories

Use trackpy to link particle positions across frames into trajectories. Apply quality cuts: minimum track length, morphological quality (bead shape), physical reliability (stable D estimate). Reject beads near walls, beads showing non-Brownian motion, beads with very short tracks.

Step 3 — Compute MSD per bead

For each accepted trajectory, compute the time-averaged MSD at each lag time τ. Check for linearity. Correct for drift if detected. Fit a line to the linear regime. Extract D = slope/4.

Step 4 — Measure r per bead

Extract the bead radius r from the optical image using the calibrated pixel scale. Note: diffraction spreads the dark ring ~1-2 px past the true edge, biasing r slightly upward for smaller beads. This bias in r propagates into kB = 6πηrD/T.

Step 5 — Compute kB per bead

For each bead i: kB,i = 6π η(T) ri Di / T. Use the measured T and the computed η(T). The result is a distribution of kB estimates — one per bead.

Step 6 — Combine estimates

The correct combination is the bootstrap standard error on the per-bead median kB,i — not a regression slope (which is broken when all beads cluster in a narrow 1/r range). Report the median ± bootstrap SE, and compare to the accepted value kB = 1.381 × 10−23 J/K.

Your target

A measurement of kB within 20% of the accepted value is considered a success for this lab. Your week-1 pooled result was ~1.07× the accepted value — within target. The week-2 temperature-dependent runs add the check that D ∝ T/η(T) as predicted by Stokes-Einstein.

The physical story in one paragraph

Water at temperature T consists of molecules with mean kinetic energy kBT per direction (equipartition). They constantly bombard your polystyrene sphere with asymmetric net forces (Langevin). The drag that slows the sphere (Stokes: γ = 6πηr) and the diffusion that drives it (Einstein: D = kBT/γ) are linked by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Measuring D from the MSD slope, r from the optical image, T from a thermometer, and η(T) from tables, you extract kB — the fundamental bridge between the microscopic and macroscopic worlds.

Extension · Diffusion-Limited Aggregation

What is DLA?

Diffusion-Limited Aggregation (DLA) is a growth process in which particles diffusing randomly through a medium collide with and permanently attach to a growing cluster. The result is a branching, fractal structure — one of the most striking examples of complexity emerging from simple rules.

Shlomo suggested this as your lab extension: metal salt ions in solution under an applied electric voltage. The ions drift toward an electrode, diffusing along the way, and stick where they first touch the growing deposit. The deposit grows outward in elaborate, tree-like fractal branches.

Why this connects to Brownian motion

DLA is not a separate topic — it is Brownian motion taken to its logical consequence. Each individual ion undergoes the same random walk you have been studying all semester. The fractal pattern is entirely a product of that random walk meeting an irreversible sticking condition. The word "diffusion-limited" in the name means exactly that: the shape of the aggregate is determined by diffusion, not by chemistry or surface energy. Remove the random walk and you get a different pattern entirely.

The canonical DLA algorithm

The classic thought experiment that defines DLA: place a seed particle at the origin. Release a new particle far away; let it random-walk until it either escapes to infinity or reaches a site adjacent to the existing cluster, at which point it sticks. Repeat. The cluster that grows is a fractal with a characteristic branching structure that looks the same at every scale — from microns to centimetres.

The physics: ions under an electric field

In the lab you will deposit metal ions (from a salt solution) under an applied voltage. This is electrodeposition driven by DLA. The physics involves three forces acting on each ion simultaneously:

Thermal diffusion — random

Exactly as in your Brownian motion experiment: the solvent molecules bombard the ion from all sides, causing a random walk with diffusion coefficient D = kBT / (6πηr). For a small ion (r ~ 0.1 nm), D ~ 10−9 m²/s — much larger than for your 1 µm spheres, because D ∝ 1/r and ions are ~104× smaller.

Electrophoretic drift — directed

The applied electric field E [V/m] exerts a force F = qE on each ion (charge q = ze, where z is the valence). This gives a systematic drift velocity vdrift = μE, where μ = q / (6πηr) [m²/(V·s)] is the electrophoretic mobility. This is the "drift" you studied in Chapter 12 — here it is intentional and controlled by the voltage knob.

Sticking — irreversible

When the ion reaches the electrode surface (or an already-deposited ion), a reduction reaction occurs: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (or Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn, depending on the salt). The ion becomes a neutral metal atom, permanently fixed. There is no detachment — the sticking is irreversible. This irreversibility is what causes the fractal to persist rather than equilibrate into a compact sphere.

Total ion displacement per timestep Δr = Δrdiffusion + vdrift · Δt = [F(t)/γ]Δt + (qE/γ)Δt F(t)/γ — random Brownian kick (zero mean) qE/γ — deterministic drift toward the cathode γ = 6πηr — Stokes drag, same as always

The relative importance of drift vs. diffusion is set by a single dimensionless number — the Péclet number, which is the subject of the next section.

What the voltage controls

Turning up the voltage increases E, which increases vdrift. At low voltage, diffusion dominates and you get a fractal DLA pattern. At high voltage, drift dominates and the deposit becomes a compact, directed dendrite — the fractal branching disappears. This is the key experimental variable: the voltage knob changes the Pe number and therefore the morphology of the deposit.

Fractal geometry of the deposit

The DLA cluster is a fractal: it has structure at every length scale, and its mass M does not scale with size R the way a compact object does. For a compact 2D disc, M ∝ R². For a DLA fractal, M ∝ Rdf where df ≈ 1.71 is the fractal dimension.

Fractal scaling relation M(R) ∝ Rdf    df ≈ 1.71 (2D DLA) M — number of particles in the cluster within radius R df — fractal dimension (2D DLA ≈ 1.71, between 1 and 2) Compare: compact 2D disc has df = 2 exactly

How to measure df experimentally: photograph the deposit. Count the number of filled pixels M(R) within circles of increasing radius R centred on the seed electrode. Plot log(M) vs. log(R). The slope is df. A slope of ~1.71 confirms DLA; a slope approaching 2 means the deposit is compact (high-voltage regime).

Why is df less than 2?

The fractal dimension being less than 2 reflects the "screening" mechanism at the heart of DLA. A diffusing particle approaching the cluster is much more likely to hit a protruding tip than to navigate deep into a fjord-like interior — the interior is screened by the outer branches. Tips grow faster than interior regions. This positive feedback causes the branching instability: every small protrusion grows faster, becoming a bigger protrusion, growing even faster. The result is a pattern where mass is concentrated on a set of fractal branches with empty space between them.

Measuring df in your experiment

Your deposit is quasi-2D (thin electrolyte layer between two plates). The fractal dimension you measure will be close to the 2D DLA value of 1.71 if the voltage is low and diffusion dominates. At higher voltages, df will approach 2 as the deposit becomes more compact. Quantifying this transition as a function of voltage is a concrete, measurable research question.

The Péclet number — the key dimensionless ratio

The Péclet number Pe is the ratio of advective (drift) transport to diffusive transport. It is the single most important parameter governing the morphology of your deposit:

Péclet number Pe = vdrift · L / D = (μE · L) / D vdrift = μE — drift velocity [m/s] L — characteristic length scale [m] (e.g. gap between electrodes) D — diffusion coefficient [m²/s] μ — electrophoretic mobility [m²/(V·s)]

Three regimes:

Pe ≪ 1 — diffusion dominated

The ion random-walks many times its mean-free path before drifting appreciably. It samples all approach directions to the cluster equally. This is classical DLA: fractal deposits, df ≈ 1.71. This is what you see at low voltage.

Pe ~ 1 — crossover

Drift and diffusion compete on the same length scale. The fractal branches become directionally biased — they lean toward the counter-electrode. The fractal dimension begins to increase toward 2. The deposit morphology is sensitive to small changes in voltage in this regime.

Pe ≫ 1 — drift dominated

Ions travel almost directly toward the electrode in near-straight lines, with only small diffusive side-steps. The deposit is a compact, directed dendrite — still branched due to the instability at the growth front, but no longer fractal in the DLA sense. df → 2.

Deep connection to Brownian motion

The connection between DLA and your main experiment runs deeper than "both involve random walks." Here is the precise chain:

Same underlying equation

An ion undergoing DLA obeys exactly the same overdamped Langevin equation as your polystyrene sphere: γ(dx/dt) = F(t) + qE. The only new term is qE — the electric driving force. Remove the field, and DLA is pure Brownian motion with a sticking boundary condition.

Same MSD, same D

Between sticking events, the ion's MSD grows as ⟨r²⟩ = 4Dt, exactly as for free diffusion. The Stokes-Einstein relation D = kBT/(6πηr) still applies (using the ionic radius r ~ 0.1 nm). The fractal pattern is entirely encoded in where the first-passage to the cluster boundary happens — not in the diffusion law itself.

Equipartition still applies

The electric field does not change the thermal energy of the ion — it adds a directional bias on top of the thermal motion. The ion still has mean kinetic energy ½kBT per direction from equipartition. The Péclet number Pe = vdrift/vthermal × (L/ℓ) compares the directed drift to the thermal speed — but thermally the ion is unchanged.

Fractal dimension from first-passage statistics

The fractal dimension df ≈ 1.71 is a mathematical property of 2D random walks and the harmonic measure (the probability distribution of where a diffusing particle first hits a surface). It is not a property of chemistry or electrostatics — it is purely a consequence of Brownian motion + irreversible sticking. This is why DLA deposits from copper sulfate, zinc sulfate, and silver nitrate all give the same df.

The deepest connection

DLA is a first-passage problem for Brownian motion. The fractal structure of the cluster is a direct physical record of the probability that a random walker, released far away, first hits a given boundary point. The tips are "most visible" to incoming random walkers (high harmonic measure); the fjords are "invisible" (exponentially suppressed harmonic measure). Fractal geometry is a consequence of the geometry of random walks in two dimensions.

The experiment: what you will do and measure

Shlomo confirmed the lab has the basic equipment. The setup is a thin-layer electrodeposition cell: two electrodes separated by a gap of order 1 cm, filled with a metal salt solution (e.g. CuSO₄ or ZnSO₄), viewed under a camera or microscope.

Setup

Cell geometry

A thin layer of electrolyte (1–2 mm thick) between two flat electrodes or two wires. Quasi-2D geometry is important — it allows you to photograph the deposit from above and treat it as 2D. The central wire or pin is the cathode (where deposit grows); the outer ring or wire is the anode.

Salt concentration

Lower concentration → fewer ions → growth is more diffusion-limited (lower Pe) → better fractal. Higher concentration → more ions → growth becomes reaction-limited rather than diffusion-limited → more compact deposit. Typical range: 0.01–0.1 M.

Applied voltage

This is the main experimental variable. Low voltage (1–3 V): fractal DLA. High voltage (5–15 V): compact/directed. Measure df at each voltage. The crossover voltage is where Pe ~ 1.

What to measure

Fractal dimension df: photograph the deposit at regular time intervals. For each image, compute M(R) — the number of filled pixels within radius R of the seed — for a range of R. Plot log(M) vs. log(R) and extract the slope df. This can be done in Python with basic image processing.

Growth rate: the cluster radius R(t) also follows a power law: R(t) ∝ tα, where α depends on the Pe number. In pure DLA (Pe ≪ 1), α ≈ 0.58 in 2D. Measuring α at different voltages is another quantitative test.

Morphology transition: the clearest result is simply the visual transition from branching fractal to compact dendrite as voltage increases. Capturing this at several voltages and quantifying it via df is a complete, publication-quality experiment at the undergraduate level.

Your research question (as discussed with Shlomo)

How does the fractal dimension df of an electrodeposited metal aggregate depend on the applied voltage? The prediction from DLA theory: df ≈ 1.71 at low voltage (Pe ≪ 1), increasing toward 2 at high voltage (Pe ≫ 1). This is directly testable with image analysis and quantifies the competition between diffusion and drift — the same competition that governs Brownian motion everywhere in this lab.

Einstein relation for mobility

There is a direct analogue of the Stokes-Einstein relation for electrophoresis. The electrophoretic mobility μ and the diffusion coefficient D are related by: μ = qD / kBT. This is the Nernst-Einstein relation — the same fluctuation-dissipation physics as Stokes-Einstein, applied to electric driving instead of thermal gradient. It says: the same collisions that cause diffusion also determine how fast the ion drifts in a field. Measuring both μ and D gives you kB again, via a completely different route.
