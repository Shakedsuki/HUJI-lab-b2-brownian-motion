Foundational for MSD analysis  · 
random variable

A quantity whose value is determined by a random process. In this experiment, the displacement of a particle over time interval \(t\) — written \(\Delta x\) or \(\Delta r\) — is a random variable. It takes a different value every observation, even under identical conditions.

expectation  \(E[X]\)

The probability-weighted average over all possible outcomes:

\[ E[X] = \sum_i x_i\, p_i \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad E[X] = \int x\, f(x)\, dx \quad \text{(continuous)} \]

For Brownian motion: \(E[\Delta x] = 0\) — no preferred direction. The average displacement is zero, which is why we must square before averaging.

variance  \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - E[X]^2\)

Measures spread around the mean. Since \(E[\Delta x] = 0\):

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\Delta x) = E[(\Delta x)^2] - 0^2 = \langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle \]

This is exactly the MSD. Computing the variance of the displacement distribution is the experiment.

normal (Gaussian) distribution  \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\)
\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

Brownian displacement along one axis: \(\Delta x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,2Dt)\), so \(\sigma^2 = 2Dt\). The distribution broadens as \(\sqrt{t}\) — the hallmark of diffusion.

connection to this experiment
In 2D: \(\langle r^2\rangle = \langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle + \langle(\Delta y)^2\rangle = 4Dt\). The MSD is the variance of the displacement. Plot \(\langle r^2\rangle\) vs \(t\) → slope \(= 4D\) → extract \(k_B\) via \(D = k_BT/(6\pi\eta r)\).

Know what assumptions each principle requires  · 
stokes' law — viscous drag

The drag force on a sphere of radius \(r\) moving at velocity \(v\) through fluid of dynamic viscosity \(\eta\):

\[ F_\text{drag} = \underbrace{6\pi\eta r}_{\gamma}\cdot v \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \gamma = 6\pi\eta r \]

Viscosity \(\eta\): resistance of a fluid to shear deformation. SI units: Pa·s. Practical unit: cP (centipoise). Water at 20°C: \(\eta \approx 1\,\text{cP} = 10^{-3}\,\text{Pa·s}\).

Assumptions required:

  • Reynolds number \(Re = \rho v r/\eta \ll 1\) — viscous forces dominate inertia. For 1 µm sphere at µm/s in water: \(Re \sim 10^{-6}\) ✓
  • Spherical particle (the factor of \(r\), not \(r^2\) or \(r^3\), comes from solving the Stokes flow equations for a sphere)
  • Particle far from walls — proximity to coverslip increases effective drag
  • Newtonian, incompressible fluid
equipartition theorem

Each quadratic degree of freedom in thermal equilibrium carries average energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT\). Applied to particle velocity per axis:

\[ \frac{1}{2}m\langle v_x^2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}k_BT \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle v_x^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{m} \]

In the Langevin derivation this is the step that introduces \(k_BT\) — it links the bath temperature to the particle's kinetic energy, giving the MSD its temperature dependence.

Why mass drops out: In the long-time limit \(t \gg m/\gamma\), the inertial term vanishes. For a 1 µm sphere in water, \(m/\gamma \approx 10^{-7}\,\text{s}\) — inaccessibly short. The final result \(\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt\) with \(D = k_BT/\gamma\) contains no \(m\).

ergodic hypothesis

For an ergodic system, the time average of an observable equals the ensemble average:

\[ \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f\!\left(x(t)\right)dt \;=\; \langle f(x)\rangle_\text{ensemble} \]

Practical consequence: instead of tracking \(N\) particles simultaneously for time \(t\), track 1 particle for time \(N\cdot t\) and get equivalent statistics. This justifies single-particle long-trajectory measurements.

⚠ this is an assumption, not a proven theorem for this system

For equilibrium Brownian motion it is empirically well-justified. Flag it as an assumption in the colloquium — Shlomo specifically listed it.

connection to this experiment
The Stokes-Einstein equation \(D = k_BT/(6\pi\eta r)\) combines all three: \(k_BT\) from equipartition, \(\gamma = 6\pi\eta r\) from Stokes, and ergodicity to justify the measurement method. From the slope of \(\langle r^2\rangle\) vs \(t\) at known \(r\) and \(\eta(T)\), you extract \(k_B\).
